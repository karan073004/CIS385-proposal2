The caseWhy the Fraser Valley needs Valley Sentinel

Most new human diseases do not start in people. The World Organisation for Animal Health estimates that more than 75% of emerging infectious diseases in humans are zoonotic, meaning they spread from animals (World Organisation for Animal Health, 2024). The World Health Organization promotes a "One Health" approach, which treats the health of people, animals and the environment as one connected system instead of separate fields (World Health Organization, 2023). If the next pandemic is likely to begin in animals, the best place to stop it is where people and animals meet most closely.

In British Columbia, that place is the Fraser Valley. The valley is home to about 80% of the province's poultry farms (CBC News, 2022), and Fraser Valley East and Abbotsford together earned 37% of all farm revenue in BC in 2018 (Statistics Canada, 2021). The same region has 324,005 residents (Statistics Canada, 2022a) and is one of the fastest-growing regional districts in the province (Fraser Valley Regional District, 2024). Dense farms, a growing population and a busy border crossing make the valley a front line for animal-to-human disease.

This is not a theory. In 2004, an H7N3 avian influenza outbreak led Canadian officials to order the depopulation of all poultry south of the Fraser River, about 19 million birds (CIDRAP, 2004). In 2014, an H5N2 outbreak on farms in Abbotsford and Chilliwack was the first time a Eurasian H5 bird flu virus caused an outbreak in domestic poultry in North America (Canadian Food Inspection Agency, 2015). In the 2023 season, 52 BC farms, mostly in the Fraser Valley, were infected with highly pathogenic H5N1 (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2023a).

The virus has already crossed into people here. In November 2024, a teenager in the Fraser Health region became the first person known to catch H5N1 bird flu inside Canada, and was in critical condition in hospital (CBC News, 2024). Officials found the virus was closely related to strains circulating in BC poultry, but could not determine how the teen was infected. Just across the border, Washington State reported nine human H5 cases among poultry workers in 2024 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024), and in October 2026 H5N1 was confirmed in Washington dairy cattle for the first time (Washington State Department of Agriculture, 2026). Each human infection gives the virus another chance to adapt to spread between people.

We already know what an unprepared pandemic costs. The Auditor General of Canada found that the Public Health Agency of Canada was not as well prepared for COVID-19 as it could have been (Auditor General of Canada, 2021a), and that long-known problems with the national stockpile had not been fixed (Auditor General of Canada, 2021b). At least 37,560 Canadians died directly from COVID-19 between January 2020 and June 2022 (Statistics Canada, 2022b), more than 32,400 British Columbians had surgery postponed or never scheduled in the first wave (CBC News, 2021), and federal emergency aid cost about $169 billion in 2020 alone (CBC News, 2020).

Current funding is far too small for the risk. BC created a $5 million Farmed Animal Disease Program in 2023 to help farmers prepare for outbreaks (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2023b), and a $2.5 million program is helping about 75 Fraser Valley poultry farms install air filtration and UV equipment (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2025a). These programs work, but they reach only a fraction of farms. The province's Animal Health Centre in Abbotsford is a key diagnostic lab for avian influenza and handles more than 10,000 cases a year (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2025b), but animal labs and human health labs still work largely apart.

Our idea in one sentence: Valley Sentinel links farm biosecurity, constant environmental testing, a shared animal-and-human lab at UFV, protection for farm workers and a rapid response unit into one system, so a dangerous virus is caught in a barn or a wetland and stopped before it becomes the next pandemic.

Item 2Scope

Scope management is used to specify and control what a project does and does not include (Project Management Institute [PMI], 2021). A firm scope prevents a $1B program from snowballing out of everything in the ag world.

In scope

  • Valley One Health Lab at UFV's Chilliwack campus, home to the university's agriculture and health sciences programs (University of the Fraser Valley, n.d.). It tests animal, environmental, and human samples simultaneously and sequences virus genomes.
  • Farm Biosecurity Upgrade Fund: air filtration, UV systems, controlled-entry rooms, wild-bird-proofing, and wash stations on 1,000 poultry, dairy, and hog farms.
  • Sentinel Surveillance Network: wetland sampling for migrating wild birds, wastewater sampling at the JAMES plant in Abbotsford (which also takes Mission and Sumas, Washington), bulk-milk sampling on dairy farms, and air sampling in poultry barns (City of Abbotsford, n.d.).
  • Farm Worker Health Program: free protective equipment, seasonal flu vaccines, rapid testing and mobile clinics, with outreach in workers' own languages.
  • Rapid Response Unit: teams and equipment to contain a farm within 24 hours, and fast compensation so farmers report early.
  • One Health Data Platform: one shared, secure dashboard used by the CFIA, the provincial Animal Health Centre, Fraser Health, and the BC Centre for Disease Control.

Out of scope

  • Modifying CFIA's statutory powers or changing federal compensation regulations.
  • Production or manufacturing of any drug (a) for animals or (b) for humans.
  • Replacing the provincial Animal Health Centre, which is its own entity.
  • Operating expenses after 31 December 2029, other than for the sustainment fund.

The largest concern for our stakeholders is that farmers worry a positive test result means the loss of their flock and income. Item 7Champion

A champion is someone in a top position to endorse your project and secure resources and support (Schwalbe, 2019). Valley Sentinel has two champions. The executive champion is the UFV president, who is in position to allocate campus land at Chilliwack, connect the health sciences and agriculture faculties, and influence government. The industry champion is the chair of the BC Poultry Association, because that is where the biggest bottleneck lies. A farmer respected by his peers telling other farmers to sign up is more powerful than any advertising.

Item 6Four frames of organizations: UFV

Bolman and Deal (2017) describe four frames for understanding organizations.

  • Structural frame: UFV makes decisions through its Board of Governors, Senate, and faculties. Because this project involves two faculties (agriculture and health sciences), we are setting up a single "One Health" steering group with the authority to avoid cracks between faculties.
  • Human resources frame: University staff want meaningful work, not more free labour. The project adds jobs, postgraduate research, and new programs, so staff view it as expansion.
  • Political frame: Government agencies, farmers, and the university all want influence. Shared governance, with a seat for each sector and jobs placed fairly across Chilliwack and Abbotsford, lessens the tension.
  • Symbolic frame: UFV describes itself as the university of the Fraser Valley, located on farms and on Stó:lō land. "Protecting the valley's farms and families" fits that image and gains backing.

Item 9Project life cycle model

Valley Sentinel has a hybrid life cycle. Our lab building uses the waterfall (predictive) approach because we have to build and acquire biosafety certifications in an ordered sequence based on approved designs. Our surveillance network and data platform use a spiral process, in which each spiral begins with a set of goals, a risk assessment, and building and testing a prototype, followed by a plan for the subsequent spirals (Boehm, 1988). This works for us because we will only find out how well the sensors and data views perform on real farms. Farm upgrades are released in three waves, so the lessons from the wave 1 upgrades will improve wave 2, and then wave 3. Schwalbe (2019) states that we need a life cycle that considers how well known the requirements are.

This second flow chart shows how Valley Sentinel operates once it is launched. Each step listed is a deliverable of the project.

H5 is detected by a barn air sensor, a milk sample, or a wetland swab.

Confirm

One Health Lab detects and sequences the virus and looks for evidence it is adapting to mammals.

Data platform sends information to CFIA, the Ministry, Fraser Health, and nearby farms.

Farms enter high-biosecurity mode through their upgraded entry rooms.

Protect people

Mobile clinics test and protect exposed workers and families.

Contain

Farm containment is supported by CFIA through the Rapid Response Unit; farmers also get a compensation advance.

Items 15 & 17Quality management, testing and cost of quality

The quality management system (QMS) will ensure the project meets the original need through planning, assurance, and control (PMI, 2021). The standards that will apply to Valley Sentinel are the Canadian Biosafety Standard for the lab, ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation of the testing, CFIA-approved testing methods, and national biosecurity standards for poultry and dairy farms.

Testing plan

  • Proficiency testing: the lab is asked to analyze blind samples from the reference labs and must reach the same findings as the reference lab before it releases its own data.
  • Sensor pilot: six months of testing at 10 farm and wetland sites; sensors must match the lab samples 95% of the time.
  • Software testing: unit, security, integration, and user acceptance testing of the data platform with CFIA, Ministry, and Fraser Health staff.
  • Farm audits: all upgraded farms will be checked before the final grant payment.
  • Exercise Flyway (September 2029): a complete, scaled-up simulation in which a decoy H5 signal from a wetland is carried through every stage of the barn-to-response process. The project cannot close until it passes.

Cost of quality

The cost of quality is the cost of conformance plus the cost of non-conformance. Crosby (1979) believed that the cost of doing it right the first time is lower than the cost of fixing failures.

Items 18 & 19Human resource management and motivation

The core team expands from 10 people in 2027 to roughly 180 staff and contractors at the peak (not counting construction trades). The additions include:

  • Program Director (PMP), six workstream managers, and a program office (scheduler, cost analyst, risk manager).
  • Lab director, virologists, laboratory technologists, bioinformaticians, and a biosafety officer.
  • Veterinarians and animal health technicians for surveillance and response.
  • Government inspectors and upgrade advisors.
  • Nurses and outreach workers (by language) working with farm workers.
  • Software developers, a data architect, and a privacy officer.
  • A farmer liaison and an Indigenous engagement lead.

It will be difficult to hire. There was an all-time high of 95,800 health care job vacancies in Canada in late 2022 (Statistics Canada, 2023), and health workers said they felt burned out from COVID-19 (Canadian Institute for Health Information, 2023). To avoid recruiting from hospitals, we train new lab and animal health technicians through UFV programs paid for by the lab budget.

How we will motivate people

Herzberg (1968) identified that hygiene factors like pay, safety, and working conditions help prevent dissatisfaction, whereas motivators like achievement, recognition, responsibility, and growth generate genuine motivation. We address hygiene factors by ensuring fair pay, safe working environments, and appropriate protective equipment. For motivators, we acknowledge teams at each milestone, empower workstreams with real decision-making authority, fund professional certifications, and link the work to its purpose: supporting local farms and protecting local families. Schwalbe (2019) further suggests that managers should understand what each individual values, leading them to conduct regular one-on-one meetings.

Item 25Closing process

Formal closure is the settling of the project, verifying that all work is approved, and documenting lessons learned (PMI, 2021). Valley Sentinel closes from October to December 31, 2029:

  • Get signed acceptance for all deliverables from their operating owner.
  • Turn over the lab to UFV, the farm fund to the Ministry, the response equipment to CFIA and the Ministry, and the worker program to Fraser Health.
  • Close out all contracts and grants. Pay all remaining invoices and transfer any unspent contingency into the sustainment fund.
  • Release project staff with recognition, and assist in transitions to permanent positions.
  • Conduct lessons-learned workshops and issue a final report for use in other farm regions.
  • Celebrate the go-live with the farmers, workers, and the community on December 31, 2029.
Valley Sentinel will be a success if the next deadly virus is detected in a barn air filter or a wetland sample, not a hospital, and is contained there.
